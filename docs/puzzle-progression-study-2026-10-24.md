# Math Mews puzzle progression study

Implementation update, 26 October 2026: the separate **Learn with the cat** mode and automatic recommendation cards have been removed. The Easy addition/fractions pilot, varied answer ranks and comparison sides, saved supported versus independent puzzle evidence and topic checks remain. The [24 October implementation and QA notes](qa/guided-learning/2026-10-24/results.md) are a historical record that includes the retired mode and recommendations. The research below records the original audit and proposals. Skip-ahead placement, untimed scored challenge variants and child/educator evaluation remain future work; the implementation does not establish mastery or improved learning outcomes.


Research and repository audit, 24 October 2026.

Math Mews would benefit most from improving the sequence and diagnostic quality of its existing puzzles. The current catalog already provides substantial variety. Its weakest point is the connection between completing a puzzle, understanding a skill, and being ready for the next lesson.

This study compares six relevant games or products, checks their documented teaching approaches against learning research, and examines the current Math Mews curriculum. Product descriptions establish what developers say their games do; they do not establish learning effectiveness. Recommendations below are design proposals unless explicitly attributed to research. No competitor was play-tested during this review, and no children were observed. The content findings come from static data and code inspection, with deeper sampling of Easy addition, Easy fractions, and Hard patterns. This is not a pedagogical review of every question.

The [19 October research](puzzle-design-research.md) describes an earlier catalog. Its inventory and retry findings should not be used as the current baseline. The current [curriculum documentation](puzzle-curriculum.md) is much closer to the implementation; the root README gave obsolete counts at the time of this audit and has since been corrected.

The recommendation is to retain topic choice, the cat, visual help, saved boss checkpoints, and optional challenges. First repair misleading answer patterns and beginner lesson jumps. Then pilot skill-based recommendations and delayed review in a small part of the catalog before extending them across all 180 lessons.

The comparison below focuses on how difficulty changes, rather than the number of levels or rewards.

Across these examples, three useful mechanisms recur: stable actions with increasingly demanding mathematics, assistance that responds to difficulty, and a deliberate connection between visual play and formal knowledge. They do not imply that every lesson should increase every difficulty measure. Easier review can be intentional. It should have a stated purpose and should not be presented as evidence that a new skill has been taught.

Learning research supports the direction, with limits. The 2021 What Works Clearinghouse elementary mathematics intervention guide gives strong evidence ratings to systematic instruction, mathematical language, representations, number lines, word-problem instruction, and timed fluency activities. These recommendations concern supported instruction for children experiencing mathematics difficulty. They do not validate an exact mobile-game sequence. In particular, support for timed fluency practice does not establish that first encounters with multi-step reasoning should have a countdown. [IES elementary mathematics guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/26).

The IES learning-and-study guide gives moderate evidence ratings to spacing learning, alternating worked examples with independent problems, and connecting concrete and abstract representations. It gives a strong rating to using quizzes to revisit content. For Math Mews, this supports returning to a skill with a fresh problem after a delay. It does not specify a universal review interval or accuracy percentage. [IES learning and study guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1).

Engagement and learning need separate measurements. In Habgood and Ainsworth's study, 58 children aged seven to eleven learned more with mathematics integrated into the game action. A separate free-choice study involved only 16 children. That is useful support for trying meaningful mathematical actions, but insufficient to predict Math Mews retention. A later experiment by Nidd and Muldner, involving complex numbers and 58 participants, found greater learning in its externally integrated condition and similar motivation between conditions. The different tasks and populations limit direct comparison. Integration is a promising design choice, not a guarantee. [Habgood and Ainsworth, 2011](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/), [Nidd and Muldner, 2019](https://repositories.cdlib.org/uc/item/8453p0gf).

There is also evidence that game learning can transfer. A 2023 randomized study assigned more than 3,600 seventh graders to four conditions; its final analytic sample was 1,850. DragonBox 12+ and From Here to There produced higher algebra posttest scores than the active control after nine intervention sessions. The substantial sample reduction, pandemic context, and older learners limit application to Math Mews. This supports measuring learning outside the game, not claiming that a popular game mechanic works for every age. [Decker-Woodrow and colleagues, 2023](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1405217.pdf).

The current Math Mews implementation has a strong structural base. The catalog contains 180 lessons, with four practice puzzles, one optional challenge, and a three-stage boss per lesson. That is 720 practice puzzles, 180 challenge puzzles, and 540 boss stages, or 1,440 puzzles per language. English and Latvian IDs match in each tier. There are 22 interaction types; 130 lessons use four types in practice, and 50 use three. The authored age bands are six to eight, eight to ten, and ten to twelve. These counts were recomputed from the JSON files, rather than copied from the README. See the [audit counts](puzzle-progression-audit-2026-10-24.json).

Practice completion opens subsequent lessons in the selected topic. Bosses count toward lesson completion but do not block the next lesson. Individual boss stages save progress. Worked visual help is available, and several interactions give directional feedback while preserving the draft. Those are useful foundations for a supportive learning path. [Catalog and unlock rules](../constants/curriculum.ts), [attempt feedback](../utils/puzzle-attempt.ts), [play screen](../app/play.tsx).

The following issues deserve attention before expanding the catalog further.

| Finding | Concrete current evidence | Learning implication and proposed action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beginner lessons combine several new demands | The first Easy addition lesson moves from 4 + 3 to 5 + ? = 11, finding pairs totaling 15, and repairing 7 + 5 = 11. | Counting, inverse reasoning, search, and error diagnosis appear within four tasks. Introduce these as separate steps, with enough supported examples to establish each relationship. |
| The opening fraction lesson reaches equivalence immediately | `v2-easy-fractions-parts-4` asks for 1/2 = 2/4 after shading, comparing, and matching. | First establish equal parts and the same whole. Teach equivalence through repartitioning before requiring a symbolic selection. |
| A hint can recommend an unsuitable strategy | `v2-easy-addition-join-1` asks for 4 + 3, but its hint asks the child to make a full ten first. | Audit hints against the actual numbers. Here, count on from four or join the visible groups. |
| Numeric answer choices contain a strong clue | Of 270 practice puzzles with purely numeric choice strings and a `correctIndex`, 194 have the smallest value as the correct answer. | A child can exploit relative size. Use distractors tied to plausible errors and vary the rank of the correct value. Runtime choice shuffling does not fix this pattern. |
| Comparison answers have a position clue | All 20 Hard practice `compare` puzzles have `correctIndex: 1`. The current shuffle helper explicitly leaves comparisons unchanged. | A child can repeatedly choose the right-hand option. Balance authored answer positions or safely swap options and their answer mapping. |
| Lesson order does not always express increasing conceptual demand | Easy addition pair targets run 15, 18, 12, 15, 18, 21, 5, 8, 11, 14. Hard pattern gap questions repeat the same sequences in later lessons. | Non-monotonic numbers can be valid review. Here, author explicit skill goals and review roles so a new title or larger number is not the only progression. |
| Completion is the unlock signal | Unlocks read completed IDs. Topic statistics store correct/wrong counts and ten recent outcomes, including a per-difficulty view. | These records cannot distinguish supported completion, independent success, and delayed retention. Add evidence per specific skill without withdrawing earned rewards. |
| At zero lives, active learning needs a separate route | Three shared lives cover practice, bosses, and challenges. At zero, checking pauses, while worked examples remain available. A free life regenerates every 30 minutes; a coin refill is available. Some matching mistakes spend lives immediately. | Lives intentionally limit guessing. Preserve that purpose and test free interactive learning tasks alongside existing worked examples. Whether depletion discourages learning remains a question for observation. |

Difficulty should be authored as several independent demands. A larger number is only one of them. For example, comparing 3/7 with 4/7 involves a familiar same-denominator rule; explaining why 1/3 exceeds 1/4 introduces a more important conceptual distinction despite smaller numerals.

| Demand | Gentler introduction | Later increase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mathematical prerequisite | Join two visible groups | Find an unknown part, then connect inverse operations |
| Number structure | Small whole quantities | Crossing ten, regrouping, fractions, or decimals when taught |
| Representation | Objects with matching labels | Diagram linked to equation, then independent symbolic work |
| Reasoning | Apply one known relationship | Choose a strategy, coordinate constraints, explain an error |
| Search | Few relevant choices | More alternatives or decoys that represent plausible mistakes |
| Memory and planning | Visible intermediate results | Plan several moves; retain a scratch area or trace |
| Reading and interaction | Familiar short prompt and controls | New story or control introduced separately from harder mathematics |
| Fluency | Untimed reasoning | Optional timed practice on established skills |

For a first encounter, deliberately change one main demand at a time. Later transfer tasks can combine them. This is an authoring rule to test, not a scientifically established limit of one change per puzzle. Preserve accessibility aids such as narration, readable layouts, and motor accommodations when reducing instructional help.

Here is a proposed addition progression. It describes skill milestones, not a requirement that every child solve exactly seven puzzles. Each milestone needs variants for practice and checks.

| Milestone | Example task | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Join | Move 3 ribbons beside 2; find the total | Establish addition through visible action |
| Connect symbols | Pair a picture of 4 and 2 with 4 + 2 = 6 | Link the action to notation |
| Count on | Start with 5 packed books and add 3 | Use a known quantity without recounting all objects |
| Make ten | Show how 8 + 5 becomes 10 + 3 | Teach decomposition explicitly |
| Find a missing part | Fill 7 + ? = 12 with a bar or ten-frame available | Introduce inverse reasoning |
| Diagnose | Choose a picture explaining why 8 + 5 is not 12 | Require a reason, not just another answer |
| Apply and revisit | Pack two quantities for a mission; solve a fresh example next session | Check application and retention |

For fractions, the proposed sequence is equal versus unequal partitions, halves and quarters of the same whole, unit fractions as numbers, positions on a zero-to-one number line, same-denominator comparison, equivalence through repartitioning, and comparisons with different denominators. An unequal split should appear early as a counterexample. Later tasks should also vary the whole and ask what stays the same. Avoid treating a denominator of eleven as automatically harder or more educational than a meaningful comparison of halves and thirds.

For number routes, begin with predicting the result of one visible move, then constructing a two-move route with intermediate totals, then choosing between routes, and finally satisfying an extra condition such as reaching a target in a fixed number of moves. Introduce division constraints only after exact division is understood. Retain undo and an execution trace. A boss can ask children to repair a broken route and then create another valid solution. The learning goal is planning with operations, rather than remembering an invisible running total.

The existing four-practice format can remain as an initial presentation, but four answers should not be treated as a fixed teaching dose. A candidate lesson rhythm is a familiar success, a guided example of the new idea, an independent variant, and an application or explanation. A child who needs support gets another example; a ready child can take a brief skip-ahead check. Keep bosses as applications of taught skills, with their existing checkpoints. Never use an unfamiliar rule as a surprise test at the end of a lesson.

A first adaptive system can be transparent and small. Add a primary skill identifier, prerequisite skills, representation, intended misconception, and difficulty demands to authored content. Separately record a puzzle's first submission, help used before success, retries, item variant, and session or review date. Existing completed IDs continue to represent earned progress. An aggregate topic accuracy should not decide readiness for every skill in that topic.

As a pilot rule, two independent successes on distinct variants could trigger an offer of the next step. Two conceptual errors could trigger a targeted hint, a simpler related example, and then a fresh check. These numbers are starting hypotheses, not validated mastery criteria. Do not label a skill securely learned from two multiple-choice answers. Look for agreement across representations and later sessions, and account for guessing. A mistaken drag, interrupted session, or slow response should not automatically lower mathematical placement. Give children understandable choices such as "Try another", "Show me", and "Something harder".

Review can start with one previously learned skill at the next session and another check several sessions later. Vary quantities and context without changing the intended relationship. If the child needs help, restore it and schedule another check. Avoid penalizing a missed day. Exact intervals and the balance of new work and review should be tuned from learning evidence and observation.

Feedback should preserve the child's attempt and identify a useful next action. For a selected pair totaling 12 when the target is 15, show the three-unit gap and ask what needs changing. For an incorrect fraction comparison, align equal-sized wholes. A hint sequence can point to the relevant feature, show a strategy, and then demonstrate a separate example. After a worked solution, give a new problem before counting independent evidence. The cat can notice a specific action such as splitting five into two and three, without making a mistake feel like disappointing the pet.

The user's clarification on 24 October establishes the purpose of lives: prevent guessing, while keeping some learning tasks available. Retain lives for ordinary puzzle submissions, bosses, and challenges. Add an always-available "Learn with the cat" route, including at zero lives, for guided interaction with the same skill. Existing worked examples provide a starting point, but watching a demonstration and taking part in a supported task are different activities.

For example, after difficulty with 8 + 5, a guided task could use 7 + 5. The child moves three counters to fill a ten-frame, sees that two remain, and connects the picture to 10 + 2. A fraction task could ask the child to partition a whole into equal pieces, with feedback showing unequal pieces before trying again. Use fresh quantities, avoid revealing the blocked puzzle's answer, and preserve the child's actions while giving targeted feedback. These activities should be untimed and cost no lives.

The proposed boundary is that guided tasks teach without awarding coins, clearing ordinary puzzle IDs, unlocking lessons, or automatically refilling lives. Completion can receive a small cat reaction and be recorded as supported learning, but it should not count as independent mastery. When a life becomes available through the existing recovery rules, offer a return to the ordinary puzzle. The child can also continue learning or leave whenever they choose. This keeps the learning route from becoming a way to bypass the guessing limit. This boundary has now been implemented; see the implementation update above.

Lives alone cannot distinguish a thoughtful error from a guess. Pair the limit with the existing draft-preserving feedback and the recommended repairs to predictable answer patterns. During testing, observe whether children use the learning route and subsequently solve fresh related problems independently. Do not claim that fewer submissions demonstrate less guessing without evidence about how children reached their answers.

Practice and bosses already avoid timers. Retain that separation. An untimed version of a reasoning challenge remains a separate proposal; it would still use lives for scored submissions. Reserve timed variants for children who understand the underlying skill.

Validation should begin with Easy addition and Easy fractions, where the prerequisite jumps are clearest. First have an elementary mathematics educator inspect objectives, mathematical correctness, distractors, representations, and hint alignment. Have a fluent Latvian reviewer examine reading demand and whether translated prompts preserve the intended reasoning. Then run short, consented observation sessions across different prior skill levels, including children who read slowly or need motor accommodations. A small usability round can expose confusion, but cannot establish effectiveness.

For a learning pilot, compare the current sequence with the revised sequence under similar opportunities to practise. Use fresh items before and after, plus a later check with different pictures or paper materials. Allocate children to conditions where feasible; determine sample size from the chosen outcome and expected effect, rather than treating a handful of observations as proof. Keep content, help policy, and reward changes separable so results are interpretable. Report departures and missing follow-up data.

| Question | Useful measurement | Misleading substitute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Did the child learn? | Independent accuracy on unseen variants and a delayed transfer check | Total completed IDs |
| Was the challenge suitable? | First attempts, specific errors, help use, and observed frustration | A single average success rate |
| Did feedback help? | Success on a fresh related problem after feedback | Repeating the just-revealed answer |
| Was the game engaging? | Willingness to choose another puzzle and return voluntarily, with child feedback | Minutes spent waiting, retrying, or in the shop |
| Are controls obscuring mathematics? | Correct explanation alongside failed interaction, observed slips | Treating every wrong tap as a misconception |
| Can children keep learning while lives limit guessing? | Guided-task use at zero lives, later independent performance, observed guessing, and exits after depletion | Treating fewer submissions as proof of less guessing, or every exit as boredom |

The implementation order should be narrow enough to evaluate.

1. Repair the opening hint, comparison position bias, and numeric distractor patterns. Review each change for mathematical validity in both languages. Keep stable puzzle IDs and earned progress.
2. Rewrite the opening addition and fraction sequences around explicit prerequisites. Label intentional review and write several independent variants per target skill. Remove the requirement to change interaction merely to meet a variety quota where it obstructs teaching.
3. Add skill evidence separately from completion, followed by simple recommendations, skip-ahead checks, and delayed review. Make the recommendation explainable before attempting a complex adaptation model.
4. Keep the life limit and test an always-available guided learning route with no life cost, automatic refill, ordinary completion credit, or currency rewards. Preserve existing checkpoints and existing credit for supported ordinary puzzles. Evaluate untimed challenge access separately, retaining lives for scored submissions.
5. Expand only after the pilot shows better independent performance without increased frustration or reduced willingness to continue. Audit the remaining topics using the same difficulty dimensions and answer-pattern checks.

The next concrete design deliverable should be a revised Easy addition and Easy fractions specification: skill dependencies, introductory tasks, variants, distractor rationales, hint steps, boss applications, and delayed checks. That would address the strongest findings while making the proposed learning progression reviewable before implementation.
